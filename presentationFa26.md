# presentation guidelines

Each of you will be responsible for a brief (“tight ten-minute”) presentation on a modest research project that relates to our collective course material but deepens or extends it in some way. The intent is threefold, helping you to:

* develop your research skills on a modest scale, in consultation with Library staff and myself if you wish
* kickstart the research process for the final project, identifying an area of interest and perhaps even a topic/argument you’d like to pursue in greater detail throughout the term
* offer a fresh perspective to classmates, practicing your presentation skills and creating some cross-pollination, learning who has expertise in which corners of the fields we’re engaging this term

⠀
I’ve created a long list of potential topics, pegged to dates on our schedule. There will be two presentations per session for the middle 8 weeks of the term. 

Presentations will likely vary greatly, given the interdisciplinary nature of DH and the polarity in DH projects between more traditional humanistic scholarship, on the one hand, and the “build and share” ethos of **making**, on the other. The below guidelines are more applicable for the former, traditional/humanistic route, but don’t let that dissuade you from proposing something that’s more grounded in making or exhibiting material in a natively digital way. 

Broadly speaking, presentations should:

* provide some basic information about the topic: this is the “lighter lift,” and, depending on the topic, it could be biographical data, cultural or literary historical background, some basic who/what/when/where of a text you’re engaging that we’ve not read together, etc.
* identify a critic or two who have made prior arguments about this topic. I don’t expect a full “literature review” or oral “annotated bibliography,” but a simple “here’s what X critic argues.”
* speculate on where future research or argumentation on this topic might go: I don’t expect you to have a full-blow “thesis statement,” since you’ll be very early and small-scale at this point, but I do expect you to think about what else you’d need to read in terms of primary/secondary sources to pursue things further, what kind/s of “research questions” you might pose for yourself, etc. In the end, in other words, you’ll give us a gesture of opening up into further inquiry rather than closing things off with a proper conclusion, the way you would in a formal conference presentation or journal article.

⠀
Presentations should:
* last no longer than ten minutes, so practice your timing: I will cut you off, in the nicest way, at 10 mins, whether you’re done or not.
* have some kind of visual aid, like a slide deck or handout. I don’t care much about the aesthetics, unless that’s your jam: I just want us to be able to follow along efficiently. Your visuals should have a brief “works cited” in MLA format at the end, so we can see what you found and how it informed your research.

⠀
You’ll be graded on:
* The quality of your research: I’ll look for evidence that you did some searches and located some primary and secondary sources that are relevant to the field and that they’re properly cited in your slides/handouts.
* The quality of your performance: This doesn’t have to be super polished, and I expect you to be very tentative about whatever new topic you’re confronting, but I do want to see evidence that you’ve prepared enough to give us a brief, organized introduction to your topic

And here is a list, subject to grow as ideas continue to occur to me, of possible topics. These are merely suggestive and are meant to be hacked/modded/ignored, depending on your interests:


1. The “cultural geography” side of DH has generated interesting projects designed to give a “behind the scenes” glimpse of things that are otherwise hard to grasp: on the macro scale, what Tim Morton calls “hyperobjects,” extensive assemblages of massive scale; on the micro scale,  more humble gizmos whose production processes often reveal much larger, more “hyper” processes at work. See [Mattern](https://placesjournal.org/article/cloud-and-field/?cn-reloaded=1) for inspiration. What are some of the biases and assumptions cooked into these projects? What aspects of ordinarily invisible/immaterial labor do they help us to see? What aspects do they obscure from our vision/awareness?
2. Focus on a DH “maker”: game designer, filmmaker, artist, etc. and analyze how they engage questions of “digital labor.” Examples might include Ian Bogost, the Molleindustria group, or Pippin Barr. You might use these as a springboard to your own attempts at “making” instead.
3. An analysis of “influencing” (or perhaps an influencer) via the theoretical vocabularly provided by Italian **operaismo** (workerism) tradition. How does the nexus of influencers, consumers of online content, sponsors, etc. speak to the rise of “immaterial labor” and the porousness of the boundary between labor and leisure in “post-Fordist” life? What aspects of “influencing” exceed this body of theory and force new categories or new models? You might look at Marx, Virno, Lazzarato, et al.
4. Attending to “attention”: the relationship between attention and distraction is an obsessive concern in workplaces, schools, homes, and in the public imagination at large. You might explore the history of “attention” (and Jonathan Crary’s work is a great starting point), the political economy of “attention” as “behavior surplus” ripe for “extraction” (Zuboff, Varoufakis, Doctorow, and Wu tackle this in recent works), or engage with projects designed to reclaim our “attention” for non-market purposes (Jenny Odell, the collective at friendsofattention.org). I’m open to an (auto)ethnography in which you explore your own or others’ attentional labor, or your involvement in the attentional experiements of friendsofattention.org or others. 
5. Prehistories of digital labor: following the approach of Pasquinelli in *The Eye of the Master*, look into earlier moments in the history of the abstraction and measurement of labor that prefigure and enable the status quo in our own moment. You might look at Charles Babbage’s proto-computers and his dream of quantifying labor; F. W. Taylor’s work on “scientific management” of labor, with its time-motion studies, visualization of work processes, etc.; or some of the ed tech midcentury experiments (especially those of B. F. Skinner) that Audrey Waters explores in her book.
6. Explore sleep and rest as a contested site of resistance in contemporary culture. You might look at books that celebrate … doing nothing, like the work of Jenny Odell or Tricia Hersey, Moshfegh’s novel,  *My Year of Rest and Relaxation*, Jonathan Crary’s more theoretical account of the assault on sleep by media in *24/7*, etc. Alternative focus: recovering “boredom” as a resistant practice. The above texts are relevant, but might also look at Benjamin’s comment that “boredom is the dream bird that hatches the egg of experience” and his writing on “distraction” in his famous “The Work of Art” essay.
7. Critical Labor Games: take a look at indie game designers (Bogost, the Molleindustria cooperative, Pippin Barr, games like Progress Quest or Stanley Parable or Cart Life) who engage themes of the alienation of labor, the dark side of “gamification,” and other aspects of material and immaterial labor. Craft an argument about what kind of critique these games mount, and how the specific mix of narratology and ludology, “telling” and “playing,” works in your examples.
8. Data visualization has been a vital practice in addressing a central problem in “immaterial labor”: how to *see* work that is embedded in infrastructures of mind-boggling scale and complexity and in flows of bodies, materials, and objects that operate on global scales. Survey some examples of such visualizations—the work of Kate Crawford is a good start, especially [Calculating Empires](https://calculatingempires.net/about), but you’ll find many other examples. You might, instead, attempt your own visualization of some aspect of digital labor.
9. How is labor organizing changing amid the rise of platformization and precarious labor? Take a look at academic work on this subject (the best macro views on platformization come from Wu, Varoufakis, and Zuboff) and/or examine activist work to help precarious workers and/or consumers (self)advocate: here, Oxford U’s [Fairwork Project](https://fair.work/en/fw/homepage/) is on point.
10. Very broad/big topic, but given the extremely white and male profile of the elites who command the great share of “digital labor,” explore instances of exploitation and/or resistance on the part of women, queer folk, and/or members of minority groups to the policies and modes of discipline that issue “from above.” Examples might include the droves of mostly female “computers” (i.e., the early name for the workers who fed punch-cards into early mainframes) who enabled the rise of computing tech; the activism of Timnit Gebru and others in the Black in AI collective around misrepresentations of people of color within AI interfaces; or the work of female critics, like Hao, Bender, and Gebru (see Hao’s *Empire of AI* and/or Bender et al.’s landmark article “On the Dangers of Stochastic Parrots”), in puncturing what are often frankly woo-woo characterizations of AIs powers by the elites who dominate AI enterprises like Anthropic, Google, and Open AI. 
11. We’ll be reading example of critical digital pedagogy in the home stretch of the course, and this topic has a wide range of research avenues. You might consider the humble “learning management system” (LMS), platforms that are so routine and boring that they tend to disappear from view. But what is the role of the LMS, actually? How might we “read” it as a cultural text in terms of critical categories like surveillance and/or the “imperial” dimension of increasingly powerful tech corporations? This broad topic might be bent in other directions as well, thinking about the library with Roxanne Shirazi et al., the longer history of ed tech with Audrey Waters, the role of AI in humanistic teaching and learning, etc.
